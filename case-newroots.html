Case study · Mixed-methods research

Housing research where the interviews and the numbers argue with each other

Data Analyst · NewRoots, Halifax, NS · Sep 2025 – May 2026

Housing and property management research generates two kinds of evidence that are easy to keep in separate rooms: survey and administrative data on one side, interviews and field observation on the other. Reported separately they produce a tidy document nobody acts on. The work here was making each strand interrogate the other.

01 · Approach

Two strands, one set of conclusions

The value of mixed-methods work shows up at the join. Quantitative analysis says how widespread something is; interviews say why it happens and what people did about it. Run separately they agree politely and explain nothing.

Quantitative strand

Collection, cleaning and analysis of housing and property management data — establishing how common a pattern is, where it concentrates, and how it moves over time. This is the strand that generalizes.

Qualitative strand

Research interviews and field observation, coded into themes. This is the strand that explains — and the one that reliably surfaces the thing nobody thought to put on the survey.

The join

Where a theme appeared repeatedly in interviews, it became something to test in the data. Where the data showed a pattern nobody had raised, it became an interview prompt. Disagreements between the strands were the most useful findings.

02 · Qualitative coding

Turning transcripts into something countable

Interview material only becomes analyzable once segments are coded to themes consistently. The count is not the finding — a theme raised by three people can matter more than one raised by thirty — but it does tell you where the weight of the material sits, and it makes the coding itself reviewable.

    03 · Communication

    Outputs for people who will not read the methods section

    The audience was research and non-technical stakeholders making planning decisions. That changes what a good output is: the visual has to carry the finding on its own, and the caveats have to travel with it rather than living in an appendix.

    Caveats ride along

    A number with an important limitation gets that limitation in the caption, not on page 40. If it is separable from the finding, it will get separated the first time someone screenshots the chart.

    One idea per visual

    A chart that supports three arguments supports none of them clearly. Splitting them costs a page and saves the twenty minutes of meeting time otherwise spent explaining what the reader is looking at.

    Quotes do work numbers cannot

    A well-chosen excerpt makes a statistic land. Used carelessly it is decoration, or worse, cherry-picking — so excerpts were selected to represent the coded pattern, not to be the most quotable line in the transcript.
